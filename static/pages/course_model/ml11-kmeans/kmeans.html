K-Means Lab

Six short activities on K-Means clustering. Each one runs the same way in class:

  1. Demo. Watch the instructor use the tool.
  2. Your turn. Use it yourself to answer the questions.
  3. Discuss. Compare what you found.
1

Watch it work

K-Means repeats two steps. Assign gives each point to its nearest centroid. Update moves each centroid to the average of its points. It stops when no point changes cluster.

  • Updates so far0
  • Next stepAssign
  • Points that switched (last Assign)–
  • Inertia–

Inertia after each step

after Assign after Update

Demo

  1. Press Assign. Each point takes the color of its nearest centroid. The shaded regions show which centroid is nearest.
  2. Press Update. Each centroid moves to the average of its points. Points whose color no longer matches the shading will switch on the next Assign.
  3. Keep going until the page says nothing changed. Watch the inertia chart.

Your turn

  1. Press Random start and step through with Assign and Update. How many updates did it take before nothing changed?
  2. Look at the inertia chart. Does inertia ever go up after an Assign or an Update?
  3. Turn on Squares. Each square's area is one point's squared distance, so inertia is the total area. Drag the centroids to make it as small as you can and write down the inertia. Then press Run to the end. Did K-Means beat you?
Discuss
  • Why can't inertia go up on either step?
  • Why is the algorithm guaranteed to stop?
  • With squares on, which points add the most to inertia?

Teacher notes

  • Assign: each point moves to a centroid that is at least as close, so its squared distance can't grow. Update: the average is the spot with the smallest total squared distance to a group, so moving there can't increase it either.
  • Inertia keeps falling and there are only so many ways to split the points, so it must stop. Once an Assign changes nothing, an Update would not move anything.
  • Squares tie to the practice question: distances 1, 2, 2 give 1 + 4 + 4 = 9. Far points have huge squares, which previews the outlier in activity 5.
  • Run to the end almost always lowers a hand-placed answer a little. If it doesn't, the student already found a stopping point.
  • The page opens with the same start each time, so the demo is repeatable. Each press of Random start gives a new one.

    • 
					
  • random_state fixes the random numbers, so the same code gives the same clusters every run. This page has its own random numbers, so its random_state values won't match Python's.

Teacher notes

				
    
					
  • Unscaled, income varies by about $20,000 and age by about 16 years, so income makes up over 99.9% of the squared distance. The clusters become income bands, and young and retired customers with similar incomes end up together.
    • 
					
  • Z-score gives each feature a standard deviation of 1, so each one contributes about half and the three groups come back.
    • 
					
  • Changing units (dollars to thousands of dollars) would change the unscaled clusters. Results that depend on the choice of units are a warning sign.
    • 
					
  • Min-Max depends on the single smallest and largest values, so one extreme home squeezes everyone else. Z-score is less sensitive to that.
    • 
					
  • Homes are the course datafile, limited to sales from $20k to $1M and under 6,000 sq ft.

Break it

			
Every point joins its nearest centroid, so the border between two clusters is always a straight line halfway between their centroids. K-Means works best on round groups of similar size.

    4. 
				

			

			

				Discuss
				
    
					
  • Why are the borders always straight lines?
    • 
					
  • Why does one point have so much pull?
    • 
					
  • Is K-Means wrong here, or is it answering a different question than ours?
    • 
				

			

			

				
Teacher notes

				
    
					
  • The border is the set of spots equally far from two centroids, which is a straight line. Curved or nested shapes can't be cut correctly with straight lines.
    • 
					
  • Long ovals: cutting across the long direction lowers inertia more than separating the ovals, so K-Means prefers the "wrong" cut. It is doing exactly what it's told, which is to minimize inertia.
    • 
					
  • Big and small: the border sits halfway between centroids, so the big group's edge is handed to the small cluster.
    • 
					
  • Outlier: averages are pulled by far points, and squaring makes a far point very costly. Past some distance it is cheaper to give the outlier its own cluster and merge the two real groups.
    • 
					
  • Other methods (such as DBSCAN) handle curved shapes, but they are beyond this lesson.
    •

Teacher notes

				
    
					
  • With K = 3, setosa is 100% in one cluster. Versicolor (39 / 11) and virginica (14 / 36) overlap, for 25 mismatches. This matches the lesson's crosstab, though the cluster numbers may differ.
    • 
					
  • The clusters use all four scaled features, so some mismatches look out of place on any single two-feature plot. Try other axes.
    • 
					
  • The profiles are averages in cm. Small petals point to setosa, the largest flowers to virginica.
    • 
					
  • K = 2 puts versicolor and virginica together, which matches the high silhouette at K = 2 in activity 3.
    • 
					
  • Without labels, judge usefulness by whether the profiles make sense, whether the clusters stay stable across random_state values, and whether they help with a real decision.
