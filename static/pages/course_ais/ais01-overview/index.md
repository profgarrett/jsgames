## Why do we care about IT?

IT is a significant cost. In Flexera's 2020 survey, companies spent an average of about 8% of revenue on IT, ranging from about 4% for industrial products to about 25% for software firms. About a quarter of that spending is controlled by business units, not the IT department. As an accountant, you will be approving, auditing, and using those systems.

IT is also a significant risk. In December 2022, Southwest Airlines' crew-scheduling system was overwhelmed by weather-related schedule changes, and the airline cancelled thousands of flights over the holidays. Its pilots' union had warned for years that the scheduling tools were outdated. The lesson: treating IT as a cost to be minimized, rather than as a capability to invest in, can have severe consequences.

## What is an AIS?

A **system** is a set of methods, procedures, and routines that work together to achieve a goal. Most systems are made up of smaller **subsystems** (i.e., a department within a college of business).

An **accounting information system** collects, stores, and processes data into information for decision makers. It has six components:

- People who use the system
- Procedures and instructions for collecting, processing, and storing data
- Data about the organization and its activities
- Software used to process the data
- IT infrastructure (computers, networks, devices)
- Internal controls and security

Together, these components serve three functions:

1. Collect and store data about the organization's activities
2. Transform data into **information** that management can use to make decisions
3. Provide controls that safeguard the organization's assets and data

The difference between data and information matters. **Data** are raw facts (a sale of $12 on 9/4). **Information** is data that has been organized and processed to be useful for a decision (sales fell 8% at the Morgantown store this quarter).

> Data → AIS → Information → User → Decision

### AIS and strategy

Strategy drives the AIS, and the AIS limits strategy. If a firm's strategy is to increase profitability, it will need data on price, volume, product mix, and production costs. If the AIS can't report cost by product, management can't manage it. Conversely, new technology (i.e., predictive analytics) can open up strategic options that weren't possible before.

## The value chain

The **value chain** links together the activities that provide value to the customer.

- **Primary activities** directly provide value to the customer: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service.
- **Support activities** (labeled "secondary" in the textbook figure) make the primary activities more efficient and effective: firm infrastructure, human resources, technology, and purchasing.

A common mistake is to treat "support" as "less important." Without HR or IT, primary activities stop. The distinction is whether the activity directly touches the customer's product or service. For example, at a tax firm, the accountants preparing client returns are in a primary activity; the accountant running the firm's own payroll is in a support activity.

A **supply chain** extends the value chain to include the organization's suppliers, distributors, and customers.

## Business processes and transaction cycles

A **business process** is a repeatable set of activities that accomplishes an organizational goal. Most transactions with outside parties are a **give–get exchange**. We group these into five **transaction cycles**:

| Cycle | Give | Get | Coffee shop example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Goods or services | Cash | Sell brewed coffee to customers |
| Expenditure | Cash | Goods or services | Buy coffee beans from a supplier |
| Production | Labor and raw materials | Finished goods | Brew the coffee |
| Human resources / payroll | Cash | Labor | Pay employees |
| Financing | Cash | Cash | Borrow from a bank, then repay with interest |

The financing cycle looks odd at first. It means the firm gets cash from lenders or investors and gives cash back later as loan payments, interest, or dividends.

Each cycle sends data to the **general ledger and reporting system**, which produces information for internal users (managers) and external users (investors, creditors, regulators).

## How an AIS adds value

A well-designed AIS can add value by:

- Improving the quality and reducing the cost of products or services
- Improving efficiency
- Sharing knowledge
- Improving the efficiency and effectiveness of the supply chain
- Improving the internal control structure
- Improving decision making

For example, a pizza restaurant's sales data shows which pizzas are most popular. The owner uses this to stock the right ingredients, avoiding both stockouts (unhappy customers) and spoilage (wasted money).

## Emerging technology

Three technologies show up throughout the textbook:

- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** uses computer systems to simulate human intelligence, such as learning and reasoning.
- **Data analytics** uses software and algorithms to find and solve problems and improve business performance.
- **Cloud computing** uses a browser to remotely access software, data storage, hardware, and applications.

## Key Terms

- **Accounting information system (AIS)**: A system that collects, stores, and processes data into information for decision makers. It includes people, procedures, data, software, IT infrastructure, and internal controls.
- **Artificial intelligence (AI)**: Computer systems that simulate human intelligence processes such as learning, reasoning, and self-improvement.
- **Business process**: A repeatable set of activities performed to accomplish an organizational goal.
- **Cloud computing**: Using a browser to remotely access software, data storage, hardware, and applications.
- **Data**: Raw facts that are collected, recorded, stored, and processed.
- **Data analytics**: Using software and algorithms to find and solve problems and improve business performance.
- **Expenditure cycle**: Activities to buy and pay for goods and services (give cash, get goods).
- **Financing cycle**: Activities to obtain funds from lenders and investors and repay them (give cash, get cash).
- **General ledger and reporting system**: The subsystem that receives data from every cycle and produces financial statements and management reports.
- **Give–get exchange**: The basic structure of a transaction, where the organization gives up one resource to receive another.
- **Human resources / payroll cycle**: Activities to hire, train, and pay employees (give cash, get labor).
- **Information**: Data that has been organized and processed so it is useful for a decision.
- **Primary activities**: Value chain activities that directly provide value to the customer (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service).
- **Production cycle**: Activities that transform labor and raw materials into finished goods.
- **Revenue cycle**: Activities to sell goods and services and collect cash (give goods, get cash).
- **Subsystem**: A smaller system that is part of a larger one.
- **Supply chain**: The organization's value chain, extended to include its suppliers, distributors, and customers.
- **Support activities**: Value chain activities that allow primary activities to be efficient and effective (firm infrastructure, HR, technology, purchasing).
- **System**: A set of methods, procedures, and routines that carry out activities to achieve a goal.
- **Transaction cycle**: A group of related business processes, such as revenue or expenditure.
- **Value chain**: The linked set of activities within an organization that provide value to the customer.
